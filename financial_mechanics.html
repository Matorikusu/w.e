Financial Mechanics & Capital Concepts

A clear breakdown of fundamental monetary forces: how capital expands, how currency degrades, and how purchasing power is retained over time.

Core Concept

Interest vs. Currency Debasement

While interest represents the growth rate of deployed capital, debasement represents the systemic erosion of the currency unit itself. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for evaluating true investment performance.

What is Interest? Expansion

Interest is the compensation paid by a borrower to a lender for using their capital over time. It reflects the time-value of money, credit risk, and opportunity cost.

When you earn interest on savings or bonds, your total balance increases numerically, compounding over successive time periods.

What is Currency Debasement? Erosion

Debasement occurs when a central bank or government increases the total supply of money, diluting the purchasing power of each individual unit of currency.

Historically achieved by reducing precious metal content in coins, modern debasement occurs through rapid expansion of the monetary base (M2 money supply).

The Interplay: If your capital earns 5% nominal interest per year, but the currency supply expands by 8% annually, your real purchasing power is actually declining despite nominal growth.
Growth Mechanics

The Power of Compounding

Compounding occurs when interest or investment returns generate their own additional returns over subsequent time periods—creating an exponential growth curve rather than a linear one.

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t)

P — Principal Capital

The initial amount of money deposited or invested before any returns begin to accumulate.

r — Annual Rate

The nominal annual rate of return or interest expressed as a decimal (e.g. 8% = 0.08).

n & t — Frequency & Time

n represents compounding cycles per year (monthly, daily), whilst t is total years elapsed.

Valuation

Nominal vs. Real Purchasing Power

The face-value figure on your bank account is a nominal figure. What matters for wealth retention is real purchasing power—the actual volume of goods and services that money can buy.

Nominal Return

The percentage gain or return on an investment unadjusted for inflation or taxes. It represents the numerical growth in your portfolio.

Real Return

The actual gain after removing the effects of inflation. Calculated approximately as: Real Rate ≈ Nominal Rate - Inflation Rate.

Macroeconomics

Primary Drivers of Inflation

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services over time. It stems from three distinct macroeconomic mechanisms:

1. Demand-Pull Inflation

Occurs when consumer demand for goods and services outpaces total supply, driving up prices as buyers compete for limited inventory.

2. Cost-Push Inflation

Arises when production costs rise (raw materials, energy, labour), forcing businesses to pass higher expenses onto end consumers.

3. Built-In / Monetary Inflation

Triggered by systemic monetary expansion and wage-price spirals, where expectations of future price increases drive current wage demands.
